## What should survive

### From the initial plan

- Prove the connection mechanic manually before automating it.
- Treat a responsive landing page as the first validation surface.
- Measure specific intents, mutual acceptance, attended meetings, and repeat
  behaviour.
- Use manual matching to learn which compatibility signals matter.
- Keep the initial software scope smaller than the eventual vision.

### From the pilot forms

- Ask people what they genuinely want to do.
- Capture time, place, preferred group size, and comfort constraints.
- Make accessibility an ordinary, optional planning consideration.
- Follow up soon after a planned meeting.
- Keep a structured operational record.

### From the market landscape

- Activity-first and IRL-first products provide useful evidence that people seek
  alternatives to browsing and swiping.
- Timeleft, Meetic, Spontacts, Partiful, Meetup, Sitch, and inclusive dating
  products are important comparison points.
- Local density and repeat rituals deserve more attention than feature-count
  differentiation.
- Accessibility should be designed in early rather than added after a product
  has hardened.

## What needs to change

### Mission and category

The source material moves among dating, friendship, activities, events,
accessibility, AI concierge, and a social CRM. That is a future capability map,
not a clear first mission.

Together needs one initial job:

> Turn a specific real-world intent into a comfortable, mutually chosen
> meeting.

Dating versus friendship can remain an intent-level choice only if the first
cohort can understand that promise immediately. Otherwise one should become the
initial wedge.

### Market reasoning

The competitive report scores the market against five capabilities selected by
Together, then declares the resulting combination unique. That establishes
feature whitespace, not demand or defensibility.

The report should not be used externally until each material figure has:

- A primary or reputable source
- A publication date
- A retrieval date
- A distinction between reported fact, company claim, estimate, and inference

Its next version should compare competitors on the operational dimensions that
will determine whether Together works:

- City and community density
- Time to a mutually accepted introduction
- Meeting attendance and no-show rate
- Repeat participation
- Trust and safety process
- Matching and coordination labour per meeting
- Venue or organiser dependency
- Participant acquisition channel
- Monetisation and contribution margin
- Data sensitivity and moderation burden

### Defensibility

AI is not a moat when the same report calls it table stakes. Accessibility is a
non-negotiable product standard, not a population to turn into a strategic
claim.

Potential defensibility must be earned through:

- Trust within specific communities
- Better meeting outcomes
- Repeated operational learning
- Participant-controlled context about what makes a plan work
- Reliable local supply and availability
- A product loop people choose to bring into their existing relationships

### Pilot design

Running London and Paris simultaneously may make a future story look larger,
but it divides liquidity and operator attention before the loop works. Start
with the community that can produce the fastest dense participation, then
replicate deliberately.

### Forms and data

The onboarding form asks for extensive personal information before delivering
value. It should be replaced by progressive disclosure:

1. Capture a real intent.
2. Ask only what is required to qualify or coordinate that intent.
3. Request optional planning needs against a proposed meetup.
4. Build a reusable profile only after repeat use makes it valuable.

Specific issues in the current forms:

- The document says there are two forms but contains three.
- Free-text age should become a clear adult-eligibility confirmation and, only
  if needed, a coarse age band.
- Typed names are not reliable identifiers.
- Dating-app comparison questions do not fit friendship-only participants.
- Hypothetical willingness to pay is weaker than a real paid pilot offer.
- “Everything stays private” overpromises when spreadsheets and manual
  operators are involved.
- “The worst that happens” understates the risks of meeting someone new.
- Safety, contact sharing, blocking, reporting, deletion, and operator
  responsibilities are missing.
- Comments such as “let them vent” and “investor proof” treat participant
  experience as extraction rather than collaboration.

## Claims that should not lead the landing page

- “The only platform combining all five pillars”
- “A generation ahead”
- “AI social concierge”
- “Social operating system”
- “Accessibility moat”
- Unsourced competitor scale, revenue, or growth statistics
- Claims that meetings will be better than dating apps before Together has
  outcome evidence

The landing page should make one honest promise and describe exactly what
happens after registration: the signal counts toward the London build decision
and confirmed registrants receive first access if Together is built.

## Live market check: meeting versus engagement

**Retrieved:** 2026-07-23

The current competitor pages validate IRL demand, but most still sell access,
selection, or a fixed event:

- [Timeleft](https://timeleft.com/) says “No bios. No swiping. No planning” and
  handles the group, venue, and logistics. It now offers dinners, drinks, and
  runs. Its strongest trust message is that everyone chose to be there.
- [Thursday](https://www.thursday.com/) sells access to recurring singles
  events and still directs users to browse events or download an app.
- [Meetup](https://www.meetup.com/) offers a large catalogue of groups and
  events; participants still find and choose the event themselves.
- [Spontacts](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.conceptworks.spontacts&hl=en_US)
  lets people browse, join, or organise activities and explicitly positions
  itself as friendship rather than dating.
- [DateSpot](https://www.datespot.love/for-singles/matchmaking) uses human
  matchmakers and mutual interest, then facilitates an introduction. It is a
  useful warning that “no swiping” and human curation are not unique by
  themselves.

Together therefore cannot differentiate merely through IRL meetings, no
swiping, activity choice, or a human concierge. The sharper distinction is an
arranged-date outcome:

> The participant chooses when; Together curates a mutually accepted person and
> arranges the place and time.

This claim becomes credible only when the operating terms, density, and
guarantee remedy are real. Until then, it is a hypothesis to test, not market
copy to ship.


## Recommended research replacement

Replace the exhaustive feature catalogue with a living evidence ledger:

| Question | Evidence needed |
|---|---|
| Will people register for this distinct product? | Confirmed London launch registrations by source |
| Will registrants later express timely, specific intents? | Product or manual-pilot submission behaviour |
| Can one community supply compatible availability? | Manual matching attempts and unmatched reasons |
| Will people accept curated introductions? | Mutual opt-in rate |
| Can Together reliably make meetings happen? | Confirmation, cancellation, no-show, and attendance data |
| Do participants value the outcome? | Comfort, would-repeat, repeat-intent, and referral behaviour |
| What makes a match work? | Structured post-meeting and operator observations |
| Is there a viable growth loop? | Trusted invitations that become valid intents |
| Is there willingness to pay? | Real paid-pilot or deposit behaviour after experienced value |
